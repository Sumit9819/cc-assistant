CAUSES & DIAGNOSIS

What Causes a High Fever?

Every fever workup screens for these six time-sensitive causes first. Physical exam, labs, and imaging confirm or rule out each, usually within 60-90 minutes.

Sepsis

Confirmed by blood culture and lactate. IV fluids plus broad-spectrum antibiotics within the first hour.

Pneumonia

Chest X-ray on arrival confirms or excludes. Severity scored to decide treat-and-release or admit.

Kidney Infection

Flank pain or back tenderness plus fever. Urinalysis confirms; IV antibiotics start on the spot.

Meningitis

Stiff neck, severe headache, or altered mental status. Lumbar puncture and antibiotics within 30 minutes.

Flu & COVID-19

Rapid antigen and PCR in-house. Antivirals for high-risk patients during flu season.

Appendicitis

Right-lower-quadrant pain with fever. CT abdomen in 30 minutes; surgical consult from the bedside.

POPULATIONS AT HIGHER RISK

When Fever Is Always an Emergency: Three Populations

For three groups, fever presents differently and the trigger for ER evaluation is lower. The same temperature that means “rest and fluids” for a healthy adult means “workup now” for these patients. Knowing this distinction can save hours of dangerous delay.

Infants 0-3 Months

Any rectal temp 100.4 F or higher is an ER emergency, per AAP. Full sepsis workup is required: blood culture, urinalysis with culture, and often lumbar puncture. Their immune system cannot localize infection.

Adults 65+

Older adults often run lower baseline temperatures. A fever 100 F or above with confusion, falls, or appetite loss can signal sepsis or pneumonia even without classic chills. Don't wait for textbook symptoms.

Immunocompromised

Patients on chemotherapy, biologics, post-transplant, or with HIV need ER evaluation for fever 100.4 F or higher. Neutropenic fever has narrow time-to-antibiotic windows. Bring a current medication list.

OUR PROCESS

What Happens During an ER Fever Workup?

Every fever visit follows the same evidence-based workup. The order is dictated by national emergency-medicine standards (ACEP, AAP, Surviving Sepsis Campaign). Total door-to-decision time for most fever cases is 60-120 minutes. Critical cases (sepsis, infants) bypass the queue and start antibiotics in the first hour.

Step 1: Triage

3-5 min check-in: temp, heart rate, respiratory rate. Sepsis screen runs automatically; high-risk patients bypass the waiting room.

Step 2: Vitals & History

Physician interview: onset, duration, exposures, immunization status, current meds. Full vitals trend started.

Step 3: Labs

CBC, CMP, blood cultures, lactate, urinalysis. Results in 30-60 minutes on-site, no outside lab transfer.

Step 4: Imaging

Chest X-ray, abdominal ultrasound, or CT scan as clinically indicated. Reading by an ED-trained radiologist within 30 min.

Step 5: Disposition

Treat-and-discharge with prescription, observe for 4-6 hours, or admit to a partner hospital. Clear written instructions and follow-up plan.

DECISION GUIDE

ER or Urgent Care for a Fever?

Not every fever needs an ER. Knowing which level of care to choose saves you time, money, and (sometimes) lives. The three options below cover the most common scenarios. When red-flag symptoms appear (see Section 2 above), the ER is the only correct answer.

Emergency Room

Choose ER when fever is 103+ F with stiff neck, confusion, trouble breathing, non-blanching rash, persistent vomiting, severe dehydration, OR for ANY infant under 3 months with temp 100.4+ F. Open 24/7, full labs + IV + imaging on-site.

Urgent Care

Choose urgent care for adults with mild fever under 102 F, mild flu-like symptoms, simple sore throat, no red flags. Limited lab capability; no IV antibiotics. Often closed nights, weekends, holidays.

Primary Care / Pediatrician

Best for established patients with low-grade fever, routine post-vaccine reactions, chronic-illness flares, or follow-up after an ER visit. Continuity of records; limited weekend access.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

Common Questions About High Fever and the ER Workup

The eight questions below answer the most-searched fever queries with the same clinical detail we use at the bedside. Each answer is brief by design so you can grab the headline in 10 seconds, and longer if you tap to expand. If your specific question is not covered, walk in or call (469) 943-2939 and ask the on-shift physician directly.

When should I take my child to the ER for a fever?

Any infant under 3 months with a rectal temperature of 100.4 F or higher should go to the ER immediately, per AAP guidelines. For children 3 months to 3 years, an ER visit is warranted if the fever exceeds 102 F for more than 24 hours, is paired with lethargy or refusing fluids, or comes with a stiff neck, non-blanching rash, or trouble breathing. When in doubt, walk in. We screen children 24/7.

What temperature counts as a high fever?

In adults, 100.4 F (38 C) is the medical threshold for fever; over 103 F (39.4 C) is high and warrants evaluation if it persists. In children under 3 months, anything 100.4 F or higher is a high fever requiring ER evaluation. Above 105 F at any age is a true emergency because brain function and organ stress accelerate above that threshold.

How long should I wait before going to the ER for a fever?

Do not wait if the fever comes with stiff neck, severe headache, confusion, trouble breathing, non-blanching rash, or signs of severe dehydration (no urine for 8+ hours, dry mouth, lethargy). Otherwise, an adult fever that persists above 103 F for more than 48-72 hours despite acetaminophen or ibuprofen deserves an ER workup to rule out sepsis, pneumonia, or kidney infection.

What is sepsis and how do you check for it?

Sepsis is the body’s overwhelming response to infection, the leading cause of in-hospital death in the U.S. with about 270,000 fatalities a year (CDC). We screen for sepsis at triage using temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, white blood cell count, and lactate level. If two or more criteria meet thresholds, we start IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour. Survival drops about 8% per hour antibiotics are delayed (Surviving Sepsis Campaign).

Why does ER of White Rock test infants more aggressively?

Newborns and infants under 3 months have immature immune systems and cannot localize infection the way older children do. A fever in this group can be the only sign of a bloodstream infection, urinary tract infection, or meningitis. AAP guidelines call for a full sepsis workup at this age: blood culture, urinalysis with culture, and often lumbar puncture. We follow these guidelines without exception.

Will my child need a spinal tap for fever?

A spinal tap (lumbar puncture) is reserved for cases where meningitis is on the differential: infants under 28 days with fever, any child with fever plus stiff neck or non-blanching rash, or an immunocompromised child with fever. We do not perform it routinely. When indicated, our ER physicians use ultrasound guidance and topical anesthetic to minimize discomfort.
