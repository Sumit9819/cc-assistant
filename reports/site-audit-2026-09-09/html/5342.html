Is Your Stomach Pain an Emergency?

Roughly 1 in 11 US emergency visits is for abdominal pain, making it one of the most common reasons Americans seek emergency care, according to CDC emergency department data. The American College of Emergency Physicians advises seeking emergency care when stomach pain is sudden and severe, lasts more than 30 minutes, or arrives with fever, repeated vomiting, or blood. Know When to Go (ACEP)

Come in immediately if you notice:

  • Pain so severe you cannot stand, eat, or drink.
  • A rigid, swollen, or touch-tender abdomen.
  • Blood in vomit or stool.
  • Stomach pain during pregnancy.
  • High fever with pain on one side.
  • Pain after a recent surgery or procedure.

Where Does It Hurt? What the Location May Mean

Pain locationCommon emergency causes
Lower right, with fever or nauseaAppendicitis. Come in now; CT confirms it on site.
Upper right, after fatty mealsGallbladder attack or gallstones.
Upper middle, boring through to the backPancreatitis or ulcer complications.
Lower left, with feverDiverticulitis.
Side or back, coming in wavesKidney stones or urinary infection.
Anywhere, with rigid abdomen, bleeding, or pregnancyCome to the ER immediately or call 911.

Location is a clue, not a diagnosis. About 1 in 13 people develops appendicitis at some point in life (NIH), and symptoms overlap heavily, so imaging and labs give you a real answer during one visit.

How These Emergencies Behave Over Time

Location tells you where; the clock tells you what. The time pattern of abdominal pain is often the strongest clue to its cause, and it is the question our physicians ask first.

ConditionHow it typically unfolds
AppendicitisOften starts as a dull ache near the belly button, then migrates to the lower right and sharpens over 12 to 24 hours. Steadily worsens rather than coming and going.
Gallbladder attackBegins 30 to 60 minutes after a fatty meal, builds to a peak, and can last one to five hours before easing.
Kidney stonesSudden severe waves that cycle every 20 to 60 minutes. Patients typically cannot sit still, unlike appendicitis where movement makes pain worse.
Bowel obstructionCramping that builds over hours to days with growing bloating, vomiting, and no gas or bowel movements.
GI bleedingBlack, tarry stool points to slower bleeding higher in the tract; bright red blood suggests active bleeding. Both need same-day evaluation.
Ulcer vs pancreatitisUlcer pain gnaws and is often worse on an empty stomach; pancreatitis is constant, follows heavy meals or alcohol, and bores through to the back.

Typical patterns only. Atypical presentations are common, especially in children, seniors, and pregnancy, which is why imaging and labs decide, not the clock alone.

Before You Arrive: What Helps and What Hurts

Do this

  • Note when the pain started and what you last ate. Those two answers speed up your diagnosis.
  • Bring your medication list and any allergy information.
  • Have someone else drive if you feel faint, dizzy, or in severe pain.
  • Stop eating and drinking once severe pain starts, in case surgery is needed.

Avoid this

  • Do not load up on painkillers before evaluation. They can mask the symptoms physicians need to see.
  • Skip aspirin and ibuprofen if bleeding is possible. They can make it worse.
  • Do not apply heat to the lower right abdomen. It can worsen a possible appendicitis.
  • Do not wait out severe pain that lasts more than 30 minutes. Time matters with surgical causes.

Abdominal Pain Emergency FAQs

When should I go to the ER for stomach pain?

Go now if pain is sudden and severe, lasts more than 30 minutes, or comes with fever, repeated vomiting, blood, a rigid abdomen, or pregnancy. When in doubt, come in. We are open 24/7.

Can you diagnose appendicitis on site?

Yes. CT scanning, ultrasound, and labs run on site, so appendicitis is usually confirmed or ruled out during your visit.

Do you treat children with stomach pain?

Yes. Our clinicians are trained in pediatric emergencies and we treat all ages, every hour of the day.

Will my insurance cover an ER visit for abdominal pain?

For emergencies, federal law requires insurers to apply your in-network rate even if we are out-of-network. As a freestanding ER we cannot bill Medicare or Medicaid, and we offer clear self-pay options.

How fast will I be seen?

Our median door-to-provider time is under 10 minutes, measured around the clock.

What if I need surgery?

We stabilize you, run the diagnostics, and coordinate a surgical consult and transfer so you arrive with imaging and labs already done.
