If you have decided to explore hormone therapy, the next question surprises most people. It is not "which hormone?" Your labs answer that. It is "how should it get into your body?" Pellets, injections, and creams can all deliver the same bioidentical hormones. What changes is the rhythm: how steady your levels stay, how often you think about dosing, and how quickly your provider can correct course.

How Hormone Pellets, Injections, and Creams Work

All three methods exist to solve the same problem: your body expects hormones on a steady drip, not in bursts. Each method approximates that drip differently.
  • Pellets are rice-sized cylinders placed under the skin of the hip in a short in-office procedure. They dissolve slowly and release hormones continuously for roughly 3 to 6 months.
  • Injections deliver a depot of hormone into muscle or fat, typically every week or two for testosterone. Levels peak in the first days, then decline until the next dose.
  • Creams and gels absorb through the skin daily. Levels follow your application habits, which makes consistency the whole game.

Hormone Pellet Therapy: Pros and Cons

The strongest argument for pellets is the absence of a cycle. There is no shot day, no pre-dose slump, no daily application to remember. For patients whose symptoms return every time they miss a dose, that steadiness is the entire point. The trade-off is commitment. Once a pellet is implanted, the dose is fixed until it dissolves. If your level runs high or side effects appear, there is no simple way to dial it back. That is why careful, conservative dosing from your baseline labs matters more with pellets than with any other method. It is also why the insertion should follow bloodwork, not a sales script.

Hormone Injections: Pros and Cons

Injections remain the workhorse of testosterone therapy for a reason. They are inexpensive, widely available in FDA-approved forms, and easy to fine-tune. If your 6-week labs show you are running high, next week's dose simply comes down. The trade-off is the wave pattern. Levels climb after each shot and fall before the next one, and some patients feel that fall as fatigue, irritability, or low motivation. Splitting into smaller, more frequent doses flattens the wave for many people, which is exactly the kind of adjustment a monitored program can make.

Hormone Creams and Gels: Pros and Cons

Daily creams and gels produce the most physiologic rhythm of all when used perfectly, and they are the easiest to stop or adjust. They suit patients starting cautiously, and women managing perimenopause symptoms who want room to titrate slowly. The trade-offs are human ones. Absorption varies from person to person and day to day. Missed applications show up as symptom swings. And residue can transfer to partners, children, or pets through skin contact in the hours after application.

Compounded vs FDA-Approved Hormones: What Medical Societies Say

Delivery method is only half the question. The other half is whether the preparation itself is FDA-approved or custom-compounded, and the major societies have taken a clear position on that. The Endocrine Society and ACOG both recommend FDA-approved formulations whenever one exists, because compounded preparations are not tested for consistency the same way.

"Pellets can cause high hormonal peaks and prolonged elevations, which are not well monitored."

Wender et al., peer-reviewed pellet safety review, 2025 | PubMed Central

Note what that criticism targets: unmonitored dosing, more than the method itself. It is exactly why baseline labs, conservative starting doses, and scheduled rechecks matter more with pellets than with any other option. Dr. Stephanie Faubion, director of the Mayo Clinic Center for Women's Health, has cautioned that there is "no real benefit" and "potential harm" in unregulated hormone preparations. The practical takeaway is not that any single method is wrong. It is that formulation, dosing, and monitoring deserve the same scrutiny as the delivery route. Ask any provider which formulation they use, how the starting dose is set, and when your levels get rechecked. Good clinics answer all three without hesitation.

How to Choose Between Pellets, Injections, and Creams

  • How do you handle routines? If daily steps fail you, creams will too. If you dread appointments, pellet reinsertions may lapse. Match the method to your real habits, not your ideal ones.
  • How much adjustment will you likely need? First-time patients often benefit from starting on an adjustable method, then switching to pellets once their effective dose is proven and stable.
  • How do you respond to swings? If you feel every trough between injections, steadier delivery is worth prioritizing. Your symptom pattern between doses is data. Bring it to your provider.

FAQs

Do pellets work better than injections?

Neither is universally better. Pellets deliver steadier levels with less effort, while injections offer more control and lower cost. Head-to-head, the deciding factors are your response, your schedule, and how much dose adjustment you need in the first months of therapy.

How long do hormone pellets last?

Most patients get roughly 3 to 4 months from testosterone pellets, and some closer to 6. Metabolism, activity level, and dose all affect the timeline. Symptoms returning early is a signal to recheck labs, not simply to implant a larger dose.

Can I switch delivery methods later?

Yes, and switching is common. Many patients start on injections or creams while their effective dose is being established, then move to pellets for convenience. The reverse also happens when someone wants finer control. Timing the switch around your labs keeps levels steady.

Are hormone pellets FDA-approved?

Some are, and some are not. An FDA-approved testosterone pellet exists for men, while many pellets, especially estradiol pellets for women, are custom-compounded. That is exactly why the Endocrine Society and ACOG advise asking which formulation you are getting before starting therapy.
