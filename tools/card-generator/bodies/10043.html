TL;DR

Heat illness at World Cup matches progresses fast, especially for fans who are not used to Texas summer. Early warning signs are thirst, dark urine, headache, and dizziness. Move to shade or air conditioning, sip cool water with electrolytes, and cool the skin. If a person develops confusion, stops sweating, or has a body temperature above 103°F, call 911. That is heat stroke, and minutes matter.

Why Is Heat Illness a Real Risk at Arlington's World Cup Matches?

AT&T Stadium hosts World Cup matches during the hottest weeks of the Texas year. Late-June and July afternoons in Arlington routinely exceed 95°F, with heat indices above 105°F when humidity rises. Even when the retractable roof is closed during the match, hours of pre-game tailgating, walking through parking lots, and any outdoor FanFest activities expose spectators to severe heat. The National Weather Service classifies these conditions inside the "extreme caution" or "danger" range for heat illness. Three local factors raise the risk further:
  • Visitors are not heat-acclimatized. Fans flying in from Europe, South America, or the northern US arrive with no preparation for 100°F days. The CDC notes that heat acclimatization takes 7 to 14 days of gradual exposure. A traveler who lands the day before a match has none of it, which puts them at materially higher risk than locals.
  • Pavement and sun reflection. Stadium parking lots and walkways in direct sun can run 30°F to 60°F hotter than the air temperature.
  • Sustained outdoor time. Counting tailgating, walk-in, the match itself, and walk-out, total time on the property on match day is commonly five to seven hours.

What Are the Earliest Signs of Dehydration?

The National Institutes of Health (MedlinePlus) lists the earliest signs of dehydration as thirst, dry mouth, fatigue, lightheadedness on standing, and urine that looks darker than pale yellow. Headache and muscle cramps often follow. By the time you feel very thirsty, you are already mildly dehydrated. Watch for any of these at a match:
  • Persistent thirst
  • Dry mouth or lips
  • Dark yellow or amber urine
  • Headache
  • Dizziness or lightheadedness on standing
  • Fatigue out of proportion to activity
  • Muscle cramps in legs or abdomen

How Do I Tell Heat Exhaustion from Heat Stroke?

Heat exhaustion looks like heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, and a fast pulse. The person is alert and oriented. Heat stroke is different. Skin may be hot and either wet or dry, body temperature climbs above 103°F, and the person becomes confused, slurred, or unresponsive. Heat stroke is a 911 emergency.

Heat exhaustion: urgent but not immediately life-threatening

Signs include heavy sweating, cool or pale clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, muscle cramps, headache, and dizziness. The person is still alert and able to talk to you. What to do: move the person to shade or air conditioning, loosen tight clothing, give cool water or an electrolyte drink in small sips, and apply cool wet cloths to the neck, armpits, and wrists. Reassess in 15 to 30 minutes. If symptoms do not improve, get medical care.

Heat stroke: call 911 immediately

Signs include a body temperature of 103°F or higher, hot red skin that may be either dry or moist, a fast strong pulse, confusion or slurred speech, agitation, seizures, or loss of consciousness. What to do: call 911 first. Move the person to shade and remove outer clothing. Soak the skin with cool water or apply ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin. Do not give fluids if the person cannot swallow safely. Stay with them until help arrives. The CDC's emergency warning page on extreme heat has more detail on each scenario and is worth saving on your phone before any World Cup match day.

What Should I Bring to a World Cup Match in Arlington?

The CDC recommends drinking about 8 ounces of water every 15 to 20 minutes during outdoor activity in extreme heat. For a typical match day with pre-game tailgating and walk-in, plan on 80 to 120 ounces total fluid. Pack a hat, sunscreen, an electrolyte drink, and a battery fan. Check the FIFA and AT&T Stadium bag and bottle policies before you go (rules tighten for major events). A practical World Cup match day kit:
  • Reusable or sealed water bottle (confirm allowed type with stadium and FIFA before kickoff)
  • Electrolyte tablets or powder (sodium, potassium, magnesium)
  • Wide-brim hat or cap
  • Sunscreen SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every two hours
  • Battery-powered hand fan
  • Light-colored, loose, breathable clothing (your team kit if it is breathable; avoid heavy synthetic jerseys in direct sun)
  • Cooling towel (soak in cold water, drape on neck)
  • Snacks with sodium (pretzels, trail mix)
  • Sunglasses

How Much Water Do I Actually Need During a World Cup Match Day?

For a full match day (2-3 hours of pre-match plus a 2-hour match plus walk-out), plan on 80 to 120 ounces of fluid total. The American College of Sports Medicine recommends starting hydrated, drinking 16 to 24 ounces two hours before the activity, then 4 to 8 ounces every 15 minutes during. Plain water alone is not enough for sustained heat exposure over two hours. You also need electrolytes.

What Should I Do If I Feel Sick at the Stadium or FanFest?

Get to shade or an air-conditioned area immediately (the concourses at AT&T Stadium are air-conditioned). Drink cool water with electrolytes if you can swallow safely. Apply a wet cloth to your neck and wrists. Tell someone with you what you are feeling and ask them to stay close. If symptoms do not improve in 15 to 30 minutes, ask stadium first-aid staff for help or leave for medical care.

What Helps Recovery After a Long Match Day in Texas Heat?

Mild dehydration usually resolves with rest, fluids, and electrolytes over a few hours. Drink water with added sodium and potassium. Eat foods with high water content such as watermelon, cucumber, or broth. The MedlinePlus reference on electrolytes explains why sodium and potassium replacement matter as much as fluid volume. For visitors and locals with persistent symptoms after a long match day (headache, dizziness, dark urine that does not normalize after eight hours), oral rehydration is sometimes not enough. Options include faster oral rehydration solutions, urgent care evaluation, or IV fluid therapy at a hydration clinic. When Should I Go to the ER vs Urgent Care vs a Hydration Clinic?

Call 911 or go to the nearest ER for confusion, stopped sweating, body temperature above 103°F, chest pain, or fainting. Urgent care fits heat exhaustion symptoms that have not improved with rest and fluids in 30 minutes. A hydration clinic is appropriate for mild post-match recovery in an otherwise healthy adult without red-flag symptoms. ER or call 911:
  • Confusion, agitation, or loss of consciousness
  • Hot dry skin with body temperature above 103°F
  • Chest pain or pressure
  • Seizure
  • Vomiting that prevents fluid intake
  • Any of the above in a child under 4, an adult over 65, or a person with heart, kidney, or diabetes history
Urgent care:
  • Heat exhaustion symptoms not improving in 30 minutes with rest and oral fluids
  • Persistent nausea or vomiting
  • Severe muscle cramps that do not relax with rest and hydration
  • Mild confusion that clears with rest in air conditioning
Hydration clinic (IV therapy):
  • Mild post-match fatigue and a "wrung out" feeling
  • Mild dehydration in an otherwise healthy adult
  • You can drink, walk, and think clearly, but feel slow to recover

Frequently Asked Questions

I am visiting Arlington from a cooler country for a World Cup match. How do I prepare for the heat?

Heat acclimatization takes 7 to 14 days of gradual exposure per CDC guidance. Most visitors will not have that runway. Practical steps: start increasing your daily water intake the moment you land, spend short periods outdoors in early-morning or evening hours to begin adapting, avoid alcohol on travel and match days, eat foods with salt, and plan air-conditioned breaks every couple of hours on match day. Pace yourself.

The match roof at AT&T Stadium will probably be closed. Why do I still need to worry about heat?

The match itself may be in air conditioning, but pre-match tailgating, walking from parking to the gates, post-match walk-out, outdoor FanFest events, and any sightseeing before or after the match all happen in full Texas summer heat. Most heat illness happens outside the match, not in the stands.

Is it safe to drink coffee or energy drinks at a World Cup match in the heat?

Both contain caffeine, a mild diuretic. The American Heart Association notes that caffeine does not significantly worsen dehydration for healthy adults at moderate doses (under 400 mg per day). However, large energy drinks combined with heat and alcohol can stress the cardiovascular system. One cup of coffee or one energy drink during the day is reasonable. Multiples are not.

Can I just drink water, or do I need electrolytes?

For sustained heat exposure over two hours, electrolytes matter. Sweat removes sodium, potassium, and magnesium along with water. Replacing only water can rarely cause hyponatremia (low blood sodium), which has its own dangerous symptoms (nausea, confusion, in severe cases seizures). A simple electrolyte tablet in your water bottle is enough for most spectators.

How long does it take to recover from heat exhaustion?

With rest in a cool environment, fluids, and electrolyte replacement, mild heat exhaustion usually resolves in a few hours. Some people feel washed out for one to two days afterward. If symptoms persist past 24 hours or worsen, see a clinician. Travelers attending consecutive match days should not "push through" a bad recovery day; build in true rest.

Are kids and older adults at higher risk at outdoor matches?

Yes. The CDC identifies children under 4, adults over 65, people with cardiovascular disease, and those on certain medications (diuretics, some blood pressure medications, some psychiatric medications) as higher-risk. Watch them closely, ensure they drink frequently, and move them to shade or air conditioning sooner than you think you need to.

I am going to a FIFA FanFest instead of a stadium match. Is that safer in the heat?

Not necessarily. FanFests are typically outdoors. The watching crowd often stands for two hours, sometimes without shade. Same rules apply: 8 ounces of water every 15 to 20 minutes, a hat, electrolytes, sunscreen, and a battery fan. Plan for the same total fluid intake as you would for a stadium match.

Should I get an IV before the match as a "pre-load"?

For healthy spectators, the answer is generally no. Drinking water with electrolytes the day before and the morning of the match is sufficient. IV hydration is more appropriate for recovery if someone becomes significantly dehydrated, or for visitors who arrive already depleted from long travel. It is not routine pre-event prep.
