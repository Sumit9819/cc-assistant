  NAD+ is the most expensive drip on most clinic menus, and the most oversold. Plenty of pages promise reversed aging and restored energy, while far fewer explain what the research has actually tested.

What is NAD+ and what does it do in the body?

NAD+ stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. It is a coenzyme found in every living cell, and your body cannot make energy without it. It shuttles electrons during metabolism and supports the enzymes that repair damaged DNA. NAD+ levels decline as people age. That observation is what drove the interest in replacing it.

What are the benefits of NAD+ IV therapy?

Clinics most often offer NAD+ for low energy, brain fog, burnout, and recovery after illness. Patients frequently report feeling clearer and more energetic in the days after a session. Those reports matter, but they are not the same as trial evidence. The honest position is that the mechanism is well understood while the clinical outcomes are still being studied.

Does NAD+ IV therapy actually work?

It reliably does one thing: it raises circulating NAD+ levels. That much is measurable and not in dispute. Whether raising those levels produces durable improvements in energy, cognition, or aging is not yet settled. Most published human research studies oral precursors rather than IV infusions, so the evidence does not transfer cleanly.

A 2025 review in Nature Aging, co-authored by more than 25 researchers, named NAD+ biology one of the more promising directions in aging research. The same review was clear that clinical proof in humans is still pending.

Why is NAD+ given by IV instead of a pill?

Anything you swallow has to survive stomach acid and liver processing before it reaches your bloodstream. A meaningful share is lost along the way. An infusion skips that path completely, which is why clinics use it when the goal is to raise levels quickly. The same logic applies across our IV therapy menu.

How long does a NAD+ infusion take?

Longer than a standard vitamin drip. NAD+ has to run slowly, and the higher the dose the longer the session. Rushing it is what causes discomfort, so the drip rate is set to your tolerance rather than the clock. Your provider will give you an expected time for your specific dose when you book.

What does a NAD+ drip feel like?

Most people notice a flushing sensation, some warmth in the chest, or mild nausea if the drip runs too fast. It is uncomfortable rather than dangerous. The fix is simple. Slowing the infusion resolves it within a minute or two, which is why NAD+ is never rushed.

What are the side effects of NAD+ IV therapy?

Common effects are infusion related and stop when the session ends.
  • Flushing, warmth, or chest tightness during the drip
  • Nausea or cramping if the rate is too fast
  • Bruising or soreness at the IV site

Is long-term use well studied?

Long-term safety data for repeated high-dose NAD+ infusions is limited. A 2026 pilot study in Frontiers in Aging tracking real-world infusions reported abdominal cramping, nausea, increased heart rate, and chest pressure during NAD+ drips, and found infusions of the precursor NR were better tolerated. That is another reason to treat it as supportive care rather than a standing prescription.

Who should not get NAD+ IV therapy?

NAD+ infusions are not appropriate for everyone. Skip it or seek specific clearance if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have kidney or heart failure, or are being treated for an active cancer. Anyone on multiple prescription medications should have a provider review the plan first. Our intake includes a medical screen for exactly this reason.

How often should you get NAD+ IV therapy?

There is no evidence-backed universal schedule. Clinics commonly run a short series of infusions close together, then move to spaced maintenance. Frequently Asked Questions

Is NAD+ IV therapy FDA approved?

No. NAD+ is not approved as a drug to treat aging, fatigue, or any specific condition. It is used in wellness settings, which is a different regulatory category and a reason to be cautious about strong claims.

Can you take an NAD+ supplement instead?

Oral precursors such as NR and NMN are widely sold and carry most of the published human research. They raise NAD+ more slowly than an infusion. For many people they are a reasonable and far cheaper starting point.

How soon would you feel a difference?

Some people report a lift within a day or two. Others notice nothing after a full series. Because the response varies this much, we suggest judging it after a defined trial rather than committing to a long protocol upfront.

Is NAD+ safe to use long term?

Unknown. Short-term infusions appear well tolerated in the studies published so far, but there is no good long-term safety data on repeated high doses.

Will NAD+ help my fatigue?

It might, if your fatigue has a metabolic component. It will not correct fatigue caused by poor sleep, thyroid disease, anemia, or a hormone imbalance. Our guide to IV therapy for chronic fatigue walks through those causes.

Is NAD+ worth trying?

It depends on what you expect from it. As a supportive option for energy and recovery, alongside sleep, labs, and nutrition, it is reasonable to try with clear expectations.
