Script

The layer the trade says decides most of retention before a frame is cut.

Third-party, 2026

What the trade does

Hook, 0–30s, three jobs: validate the click, raise the stakes, open the first curiosity loop. Damage starts past 15–20 seconds without value. Strong hooks hold 80–90% through 0:30.

Body in modules. Each has a mini-hook, the content, a transition. Something changes every 30–60 seconds. Two to four open loops per video — and failing to close one destroys trust.

Documentary shape: open at the most dramatic moment, then “to understand how we got here…”. Wendover: “why this matters now” inside 45s, inverted pyramid, dense stretches capped at 90s. Harris: script and visual direction written together, colour-coded for assets and fact-checks; 188-page research doc for one video.

Transitions are causal or adversative (“but”, “so”), never “okay, so…”. Payoff is the final 10%: recap, one further insight, callback.

Where we stand

The penny script opens on the claim (four times its value) and punches in sentence two. Good.

Twelve of twenty paragraphs are punch lines — the variance the rhythm metric wanted.

One open loop (the unsigned bill), closed at the end. No mini-hooks at section starts; the section titles do that work visually but not in the words.

Research is NotebookLM-verified, which caught a false causal claim before voice. That is the Harris fact-check column, done by a tool.

What changes

  • A mini-hook line at the top of each section — one sentence that says why this part matters before it starts.
  • A second open loop planted early and paid off at the turn (the 74s section change), so the middle has something owed to it.
  • Keep the punch-line density; it is doing the pacing work the planner cannot.

Narration

Why AI voice reads as robotic, and the fixes practitioners apply.

GoogleMeasuredThird-party

What the trade does

“Ninety percent of robotic is text that never got any pauses.” Write for the ear: 2–3 short lines instead of one long sentence, one idea per sentence, fragments allowed.

Pauses by role: 150–300 ms at clause breaks, 400–500 ms between sentences, 700–900 ms after a punch line, 1000–1500 ms at section changes.

Emphasis: 1–2 words a sentence, on verbs and numbers, never more than 3. Rate by block: 90–95% for complex ideas, 85% for dramatic beats, 105–110% for lists. Pitch: leave alone, or drop 1–2 semitones for gravity.

Export WAV or 320 kbps, 44.1/48 kHz, mono. Breaths matter on anything past a minute.

Where we stand

Gemini TTS chosen on measurement: pitch range 15.0 vs 9.7 semitones, loudness spread 15.0 vs 11.5 dB, 113 wpm vs 155. Free on the free tier (Google’s pricing page), reportedly 3 req/min, 15/day — three videos a day at our five chunks.

Our pause_after already scales holds by role: 1.7s at section ends, 1.4s after a short claim, 0.55s default. Close to the trade’s ladder.

No emphasis or rate control yet. One voice throughout.

What changes

  • Move to gemini-3.1-flash-tts-preview: inline tags ([very slow], [whispers]), section-scoped style, two speakers.
  • Mark one stressed word per punch line — the number or the verb — and slow the dramatic beats to ~85%.
  • A second voice for quoted material (the bill, the Mint’s own words). Variety without borrowing a recognisable house style.
  • Batch paragraphs into fewer requests to stay under 15/day.

Music

What exists, what it costs, what we may use.

GoogleUnknownThird-party

What the trade does

Voice is mixed first; the bed sits 15–20 dB under dialogue (some engineers 18–25). Under a voiceover, a generated bed is functionally identical to a licensed one; the audience is listening to the voice.

Content ID is the risk, not quality. Owned catalogues (Epidemic) are safelisted; AI music has produced false-positive claims for creators. Copyright status of AI music is unsettled in 2026.

Lyria 3 is a Create music tool in the Gemini app since 18 Feb 2026: 30s tracks, up to 3 min on Pro for AI Plus/Pro/Ultra, vocals off by choice, MP3 download, SynthID on all of it. The API has no free tier: $0.04 a clip, $0.08 a song.

Where we stand

You have the tool — confirmed in your own UI. The Python client on the same account returns prose for every phrasing, including with the Pro model named; the session then expired (UNAUTHENTICATED) and re-auth needs your sign-in.

Meanwhile Gemini wrote a score and fvs/score.py synthesises it: C2/G2 drone, tritone sawtooth resolving to a fourth at the story’s turn, 800 Hz press tick. Measured 15.2 dB under speech. Original — nothing for Content ID to match.

Rights on Lyria output: Google claims no ownership of generated content and permits commercial use for text, images and code. No Google page says it for music. Trade press paraphrases “personal and commercial use” without a quote.

What changes

  • Default stays the synthesised bed. Free, original, tuned, safe from Content ID.
  • After you sign in: one more probe with the Pro model. If the client still will not route, the honest path to the app’s Lyria is driving the real browser profile (Chrome closed), not more cookie work.
  • If a Lyria track ships, the disclosure toggle is on anyway; treat commercial rights as “likely, unconfirmed for music” and keep the synthesised bed as the fallback.

Sound design

Third-partyMeasured

What the trade does

Order: voiceover, then music, then effects under both. Hard effects first, design effects (whooshes, risers, signatures) second, atmospheres last.

“A rise should pull the audience forward, not hide the line that explains the story” — high-pass it, automate it, or shorten it when the voice leads.

Wendover: surgical placement — a distinct click on a key fact, low drones under looming systems, speech clarity above everything.

Where we stand

Cues only in the gaps, never under speech — measured earlier: a hit under narration moved the level 0.1 dB, inaudible. Twelve cues on the last render, riser + impact at sections, tick at stats.

All synthesised in ffmpeg; no library, no licence.

What changes

  • Nothing structural. Add one signature sound for this channel — a coin-on-wood tick — so the sound is ours the way the board is.

Cut rhythm

Third-partyMeasured

What the trade does

Breathing: three or four short shots, then one long one. Inhale, exhale.

Tighter early: visual resets every 10–20s in the intro, widening to 25–40s once hooked. Uniform clip length is named as the cause of monotony; deliberate fast/slow patterns are the sophisticated version.

Documentary realism justifies 6–9 second holds. Modern film averages 4–6s; the editor’s job is to deviate from the baseline on purpose.

Where we stand

Our gate buckets shot lengths in 1.5s bands and compares histograms. A uniform speed-up moved nothing; the rewrite’s short/long paragraphs did. 13 punch-ins, 8 holds, shots from 1.0 to 12.0s.

First-minute mean 2.8s on the fast plan, 4.2s on medium — the trade’s early-reset window either way.

What changes

  • Make the planner’s open-tight window explicitly 10–20s resets for the first minute and let holds run to 7–9s in sections — the breathing pattern by rule rather than by luck.

On-screen text

Third-party

What the trade does

One primary message per beat. More than that, reveal progressively. Sync by phrase, not by syllable — word-by-word bouncing reads as frantic.

Stress the words that carry meaning: the outcome, the constraint, the warning, the number. Those get more screen time and stronger motion. “Treat type motion like a drumbeat.”

End in stillness: let the final phrase sit for a beat.

Vox: the yellow highlighter sweep across the spoken phrase; lower thirds that slide, or reveal through a jagged mask; graphics on twos so they read handmade.

Where we stand

Twelve cards, each one message. Stats count up; sections slide; emphasis fades. Shorts captions already sync per word with a gold hit on the spoken one.

No phrase-level text in the long-form body; no highlighter; cards render at full frame rate.

What changes

  • A highlighter-sweep card: the spoken phrase set in type, swept in gold as it is said, using the word timings we already have.
  • Render motion cards on twos — 12 fps into the 24 fps edit.
  • Hold the last card of each section a beat longer, motionless.

Visual identity

Third-party

What the trade does

Polymatter: structured chapters plus recurring visual motifs, consistent language over personality. Wendover: one visual metaphor per system, kept graphically identical across scenes; labels that follow subjects and fade with the narration; data “quantified contextually” — linked to physical intuition, not just stated.

Thumbnails are their own design system: shared fonts, palette and layout, varied enough that videos stay distinct.

The evidence board is a stock true-crime device, sold as templates.

Where we stand

A house grade, one type family for cards, a halftone print language for thumbnails, and the cork board as the set for every generated still. The board earns its place because the thesis is an investigation.

“Four pennies in, one out” is the physical-intuition move Wendover describes.

What changes

  • Write the identity down as a system: grade, faces, card kinds, board rules (one board, few pins, no fingerprints or X marks, never the thumbnail), the halftone thumbnail palette. One page, so every video is unmistakably the same channel and never the same video.

Footage

Third-partyMeasured

What the trade does

Archives over stock when stock feels repetitive: NASA, the Internet Archive (about 65% public domain), newsreel collections used by BBC and NatGeo. Verify the licence at the original source, every clip. B-roll sourcing is named as the production bottleneck.

Where we stand

Pexels/Pixabay for motion, Wikimedia Commons for public-domain objects, Gemini stills only in Polaroids. Text ranking is blind to the picture — “money counting machine” returned rupees; vision QA rejected 0 of 114. Rule: nationality, currency and era go in the query.

What changes

  • Add archive.org and the Library of Congress to stills.search beside the Met and NASA. For a US policy channel, federal archives are the deepest free well there is.

Packaging and testing

GoogleThird-party

What the trade does

YouTube’s own Test & Compare: up to three titles and/or thumbnails; the winner is the combination with the highest watch time, not raw CTR; no winner if the variants barely differ or impressions are low. Test one variable at a time — face vs none, then contrast, then text length — and prioritise new uploads.

Thumbnail text says what the title cannot; a strong thumbnail with a mismatched title still underperforms. Systematic testing is credited with 20–40% CTR gains (third-party).

Where we stand

Two candidates staged (hybrid plate + our type; Gemini’s own). Title chosen to create tension the thumbnail resolves. Nothing published, so nothing tested.

What changes

  • Ship three variants per video and let the platform choose; log the winner and the variable it isolated in DECISIONS.md, so packaging learns per video.

The feedback loop

Third-party

What the trade does

Read the retention curve as eight named patterns. Cliff-and-slide (34% of underperformers): 15–30% lost in the first 30s → rewrite the hook, value inside 15s. Valley (18%): dip at 25–40% of length → add stakes or a perspective shift near the quarter mark. Step-down (15%): drops at regular intervals → weak transitions. Slow bleed (8%): low information density. End drop: outro under 20s, add a bonus insight. Spikes: study and repeat.

The claim to remember: most problems are script-level, decided before recording. Method: ten videos, find the recurring pattern, fix it across the next 3–5, measure, repeat.

Where we stand

No published video, so no curve. The gate measures what it can before publish — duplicate footage, rhythm, substantive content — and those numbers moved when the work did.

What changes

  • Once the first video is live: a retention log per video — each dip’s timestamp and the sentence being spoken — read against the eight patterns. That log becomes the next script’s checklist.

Policy

Third-party, July 2026

What the trade does

16 July 2026: three non-monetisable categories — template-based repetition, emotionally manipulative formulas, and AI personas advising on health, finance, law or politics. Judged across the whole catalogue, not one upload. Enforcement ladder: limited ads, suspended monetisation, termination (16 channels, 35M subscribers, January 2026).

“AI is not the problem, absence of value is.” Faceless channels with strong writing are named as unaffected.

Where we stand

No persona gives advice; every video is researched and distinct; the synthetic flag is enforced by fvs package. SynthID is in our narration, our stills and any Lyria track, so the disclosure toggle is not optional.

What changes

  • Nothing. Keep the catalogue varied on purpose — different structures, different card mixes — because the judgement is across videos, not within one.
